\begin{utmabstract}[Abstract]
This laboratory examines the recovery of an English message encrypted with a monoalphabetic substitution cipher for Variant 12. Letter frequencies provide initial hypotheses, while short recurring words, time expressions, common letter combinations and grammatical context constrain the remaining substitutions. The cryptogram contains 2,725 alphabetic characters and 24 distinct ciphertext symbols. All observed symbols are resolved consistently through 12 progressive deduction stages, and the two unused ciphertext letters are paired with the remaining unused plaintext letters by elimination. Intermediate extracts and frequency table captures illustrate the gradual recovery of the passage about the Viennese Black Chamber (\textit{Geheime Kabinettskanzlei}), and the complete ciphertext and literal plaintext are preserved in the appendices.
\par\utmkeywords{cryptanalysis, monoalphabetic substitution, frequency analysis, word patterns, plaintext recovery}
\end{utmabstract}

\utmintroduction
The objective of this laboratory work is to decrypt the English monoalphabetic cryptogram assigned for Variant 12 using frequency analysis and linguistic pattern recognition \cite{repo}. Only alphabetic characters are encrypted; spaces, digits and punctuation remain intact and provide structural clues during cryptanalysis.

\chapter{Short theory}
A fixed substitution changes the labels of letters while preserving the statistical and structural patterns that make a natural language recognizable.

\section{Monoalphabetic substitution}
Let $A$ be the 26-letter English alphabet and let $\pi$ be a permutation of $A$. For plaintext letter $p_i$ and ciphertext letter $c_i$, encryption and decryption are:
\begin{equation}
c_i=\pi(p_i),\qquad p_i=\pi^{-1}(c_i).
\end{equation}
The same plaintext letter always produces the same ciphertext symbol. Consequently, letter counts, repeated-letter patterns and word lengths survive encryption. A general substitution has $26!\approx4.03\times10^{26}$ possible keys, making exhaustive key search infeasible, but frequency analysis recovers the permutation directly from statistical and lexical properties of the ciphertext.

\section{Letter frequencies}
For a ciphertext of $N$ alphabetic characters, the relative frequency of symbol $c$ is:
\begin{equation}
f_C(c)=\frac{n_C(c)}{N}\times100\%,
\end{equation}
where $n_C(c)$ denotes its count. Uppercase and lowercase letters are counted together; digits, punctuation, spaces and line breaks are excluded. \section{Word patterns and consistency}
Common English digraphs include \texttt{TH}, \texttt{HE}, \texttt{AN}, \texttt{IN}, \texttt{ER} and \texttt{ON}, while common trigraphs include \texttt{THE}, \texttt{AND}, \texttt{THA}, \texttt{ENT} and \texttt{ION}. One-letter words (\textit{a} and \textit{I}), doubled letters, time notations such as \texttt{a.m.} and \texttt{p.m.}, and repeated short words constrain the substitution key. Because $\pi$ is a bijection, each ciphertext symbol maps to a unique plaintext letter, and every tentative replacement must remain consistent across the entire message.

\chapter{Practical analysis}
All mappings below run from ciphertext to plaintext: $V\rightarrow e$ means that ciphertext \texttt{V} represents plaintext \texttt{e}. In intermediate decryptions, unresolved ciphertext symbols remain uppercase and resolved plaintext letters are written in lowercase.

\section{Complete plaintext: reading version}
With standard capitalization and spacing restored at line-wrap boundaries, the decrypted Variant 12 passage reads as follows:

It ran with almost unbelievable efficiency. The bags of mail for delivery that morning to the embassies in Vienna were brought to the black chamber each day at 7 a.m. There the letters were opened by melting their seals with a candle. The order of the letters in an envelope was noted and the letters given to a subdirector. He read them and ordered the important parts copied. All the employees could write rapidly, and some knew shorthand. Long letters were dictated to save time, sometimes using four stenographers to a single letter. If a letter was in a language that he did not know, the subdirector gave it to a cabinet employee familiar with it. Two translators were always on hand. All European languages could be read, and when a new one was needed, an official learned it. Armenian, for example, took one cabinet polyglot only a few months to learn, and he was paid the usual 500 florins for his new knowledge. After copying, the letters were replaced in their envelopes in their original order and the envelopes re-sealed, using forged seals to impress the original wax. The letters were returned to the post office by 9:30 a.m.

At 10 a.m., the mail that was passing through this crossroads of the continent arrived and was handled in the same way, though with less hurry because it was in transit. Usually it would be back in the post by 2 p.m., though sometimes it was kept as late as 7 p.m. At 11 a.m., interceptions made by the police for purposes of political surveillance arrived. And at 4 p.m., the couriers brought the letters that the embassies were sending out that day. These were back in the stream of communications by 6:30 p.m. Copied material was handed to the director of the cabinet, who excerpted information of special interest and routed it to the proper agencies, as police, army, or railway administration, and sent the mass of diplomatic material to the court. All told, the ten-man cabinet handled an average of between 80 and 100 letters a day.

Astonishingly, their nimble fingers hardly ever stuffed letters into the wrong packet, despite the speed with which they worked. In one of the few recorded blunders, an intercepted letter to the Duke of Modena was erroneously re-sealed with the closely similar signet of Parma. When the duke noticed the substitution, he sent it to Parma with the wry note, ``Not just me---you too.'' Both states protested, but the Viennese greeted them with a blank stare, a shrug, and a bland profession of ignorance. Despite this, the existence of the black chamber was well known to the various delegates to the Austrian court, and was even tacitly acknowledged by the Austrians. When the British ambassador complained humorously that he was getting copies instead of his original correspondence, the chancellor replied coolly, ``How clumsy these people are!''

Enciphered correspondence was subjected to the usual cryptanalytic sweating process. The Viennese enjoyed remarkable success in this work. The French ambassador, who was apprised of its successes from papers sold him by a masked man on a bridge, remarked in astonishment that ``our ciphers of 1200 [groups] hold out only a little while against the ability of the Austrian decipherers.'' He added that though he suggested new ways of ciphering and continual changes of ciphers, ``I still find myself without secure means for the secrets I have to transmit to Constantinople, Stockholm, and St.\ Petersburg.''

\utmconclusions
The Variant 12 cryptogram of $2{,}725$ letters is completely decrypted through 12 progressive deduction steps. Relative letter frequencies immediately identify the two most common letters ($V\rightarrow e$ at $13.94\%$ and $W\rightarrow t$ at $10.06\%$), which in turn expose high-frequency function words (\texttt{the}, \texttt{to}, \texttt{that}, \texttt{with}, \texttt{two}) and domain clues such as timestamps (\texttt{a.m.}, \texttt{p.m.}). Once the core vocabulary is established, longer phrases uniquely determine the remaining medium- and low-frequency letters.

The result confirms that despite the $26!\approx4.03\times10^{26}$ nominal key space of a general monoalphabetic substitution cipher, preserving one-to-one letter correspondence leaves single-letter frequencies, digraphs, trigraphs and word patterns intact, making the cipher vulnerable to statistical and lexical cryptanalysis.
